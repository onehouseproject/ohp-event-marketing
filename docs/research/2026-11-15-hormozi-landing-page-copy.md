## Short answer

Hormozi would not start with the headline. He would start with the offer, because his books rank market first, offer second and persuasion third [offers-p55], and the Stay & Play offer is stronger than the page lets it look. These are the changes he would push hardest, ranked by expected effect on ticket sales **(the ranking is my inference; each reason is cited)**.

1. **Show the value math on Stay & Play.** Two VIP admissions cost $396 at the page's own prices ($198 each, in the Tickets section). Stay & Play is $299 for both admissions plus a king room with room tax. The page never puts those numbers side by side. Hormozi's whole pricing case is that people buy when what they get clearly exceeds what they pay [offers-p64], and he builds that gap by stacking named, priced components rather than by cutting price [offers-p160] [offers-p162]. Give the visitor the stack, a total value, and a reason the package costs less than its parts [leads-p69].
2. **Make the scarcity real, specific and visible, and add a dated deadline.** "Launch packages available until they are all booked" has no number and no date. Hormozi says the easiest ethical scarcity is the honest limit, stated as a number or a percentage of capacity [offers-p148] [offers-p149], and that deadlines drive decisions, provided they are real [offers-p154] [offers-p155]. The Event has real limits (a guest cap, a room block, a launch allocation); the VSL script already states a 250-guest cap. Put them on the page, with a date when the launch price ends.
3. **Add proof.** The page has no photos of a past One House Project event, no attendee words, no count of who has booked, and no picture of the actual ballroom or room. His Proof Checklist puts it bluntly: the promise does not set you apart, the proof does [proof-p7], and other people saying it beats you saying it [proof-p13]. The May golf fundraiser, the hotel, the sponsor and the host are all available proof.
4. **Rebuild the first screen for cold Meta traffic.** Keep the "handled" promise, but add who it is for, what it costs, and a button that says what happens next. His hooks are a call-out plus a condition for value [hooks-p6] [hooks-p7]; his calls to action say what to do, why now and what happens next, clear over clever [goated-p23] [goated-p24]. This matters more here because the Givebutter checkout opens a ticket list, not the chosen ticket.
5. **Make every promise match across ad, video and page.** The VSL script promises breakfast and late checkout and avoids "unlimited drinks"; the page says "Unlimited food & drinks" and never mentions breakfast. Hormozi wants the page to deliver exactly what the ad promised [leads-p213], and warns that set expectations decide whether customers feel delighted or let down [leads-p268].
6. **State the ticket policy boldly, with a reason, and add a cheap risk reverser.** The page is silent on refunds. Hormozi says to hit the guarantee hard even when there is none, and to give the reason [offers-p170]. "All sales final" works when it comes with a believable reason [offers-p172] [offers-p181]. His own VIP page states its no-refund policy plainly in the FAQ (launch page 2).
7. **Ask every buyer who else is coming, and build the post-purchase steps.** In his Leads book, one salesman beat every ticket record at an event by asking each buyer who else should come; half his sales were referrals [leads-p278]. His own launch thank-you page adds a calendar link, a "screenshot this" card and a referral prize push (launch page 3).
8. **Add a premium anchor above Stay & Play.** A clearly premium option at several times the price makes the main offer look like the better deal and sells to the few who want the best [pricing-p54] [pricing-p55] [mm-p86] [mm-p88]. For a fundraiser this is a sponsor or "high roller" table **(inference about form)**.
9. **Run a short, dated email and text push to the warm list.** The May golf supporters already have first access. His Fast Cash sequence for warm lists is a primer, an announcement, a countdown with a new bonus each step, sales updates and a sold-out notice [fastcash-p13] [fastcash-p14].
10. **Name the offer deliberately and test the names and hooks.** His MAGIC naming formula [offers-p188] to [offers-p190] and his "write many hooks, keep the winners" process [hooks-p11] [goated-p8] are how the ads and the page stay fresh in a small local market, where offers tire fastest [offers-p195].

## Style guide

Each rule gives what to do, the book's reason, the citation and a one-line example for a charity event ticket.

### 1. Offer before copy

- **Do:** fix the offer first: what the buyer gets, the price, the terms. Then write.
- **Why:** he ranks a hungry market above offer strength above persuasion skill; a great offer in a normal market works even with weak persuasion [offers-p55]. An offer is "the goods and services you agree to give", how you take payment and the terms [offers-p28].
- **Example:** before rewriting the headline, decide whether Stay & Play includes breakfast and late checkout, and say so.

### 2. Sell the dream outcome, then attack effort and time

- **Do:** name the result the buyer wants, then show how little effort and waiting it takes.
- **Why:** value rises with the dream outcome and the buyer's belief it will happen, and falls with time delay and effort [offers-p80]. He now spends most of his effort on the bottom of that equation, because big claims are easy to copy and convenience is not [offers-p81]. Value counts only when the buyer perceives it [offers-p82].
- **Example:** "Dinner, drinks, the party and your room, booked in one checkout. You just show up."

### 3. Frame the benefit through other people's eyes

- **Do:** show how the buyer looks to a partner, friends or kids.
- **Why:** people value what raises their status, and copy gets stronger when it describes how others will see the buyer [offers-p85] [offers-p86]. In ads he spells this out as What, Who and When: the value elements, the people who notice, and past, present and future [leads-p205] [leads-p207].
- **Example:** "The year your friends ask how you pulled off New Year's Eve."

### 4. Headline and hook: call out, then promise value

- **Do:** open with a call-out the right person recognises (a label, a yes-question or an if-then), then a promise.
- **Why:** a hook needs a call-out and a condition for value [hooks-p6] [hooks-p7]. Local labels work best, and the more local the better [leads-p198]. The headline is where most of the ad's money is spent [leads-p197]. In the Larry King story, only the opening sentence changed, from flattering the guest to speaking to the viewer, and the infomercial went from failing to selling millions [hooks-p4] [hooks-p5].
- **Example:** "Huntsville couples: New Year's Eve is handled. Casino night, dinner, drinks and a room upstairs."

### 5. Match the hook to how aware the audience is

- **Do:** lead warm, aware audiences with the offer; lead colder audiences with proof, promise, the pain or curiosity.
- **Why:** he maps hooks to awareness levels: most aware gets offer-driven hooks, product-aware gets proof, solution-aware gets the promise, problem-aware gets pain, unaware gets curiosity [goated-p11] [goated-p12]. When unsure, go a little broader [goated-p14].
- **Example (warm golf list):** "Golf supporters: Stay & Play for two, $299, first dibs ends [date]." **Example (cold):** "Still no plan for New Year's Eve? Here is the one that comes with a room."

### 6. Ad structure: call-out, value, call to action

- **Do:** build every ad from those three parts, and make most of the effort the first seconds.
- **Why:** his ad formula is call-outs, value elements and a call to action [leads-p196] [leads-p214]. His ads got far more effective once he focused on the first five seconds [leads-p197]. He assembles ads from parts: many hooks, a few bodies, one to three calls to action [goated-p8], with 80 percent of prep on hooks [goated-p9]. His checklist adds similar-looking people, movement, captions and a format native to the platform [adsbook-p120].
- **Example:** hook "Never played blackjack? Perfect." body: the host on the casino floor showing the room upstairs; CTA "Tap below to book Stay & Play."

### 7. Message match from ad to page

- **Do:** carry the ad's promise, words and look onto the page.
- **Why:** people click because the ad promised a benefit; deliver that same promise, avoiding a "Frankenstein" mix of looks [leads-p213]. His checklist lists a call to action congruent with the hook [adsbook-p122]. His scheduling pages start with a header confirming the visitor is in the right place [nurture-p17].
- **Example:** an ad built on the $6,000 kitchen design should land on a page whose first screen mentions the kitchen design.

### 8. Value stacking and bonuses

- **Do:** break the offer into named parts, give each a price and a reason, and present them one after another.
- **Why:** a single offer is worth less than the same offer split into stacked parts [offers-p159]. Stacking raises value instead of cutting price [offers-p160]. Each bonus should carry a benefit in its name, say what it is and how it helps, have proof, and have a price tag [offers-p161] [offers-p162]. Add bonuses instead of discounting the core offer [offers-p160]. Partner businesses can supply bonuses for the exposure [offers-p163].
- **Example:** "Two VIP admissions ($396 at the door). King room with room tax ([rate]). Total value [$X]. Yours for $299."

### 9. Proof

- **Do:** show, in numbers, from people who look like the buyer, and recent.
- **Why:** perceived likelihood of achievement is one of the four value drivers; you raise it with messaging, proof and guarantees [offers-p87]. His Proof Checklist ranks: show over tell, others saying it over you saying it, people identical to the buyer, personal over generic, big results, newer proof, more proof, numbers, third-party verification [proof-p12] to [proof-p18] [proof-p19] [proof-p20]. In-person events are where he captures his best proof: stage shots, testimonials and quick interviews [machine-p15] to [machine-p19].
- **Example:** "Photos from our May golf fundraiser" with three named attendee quotes and the hotel's name.

### 10. Scarcity

- **Do:** state a real, numbered limit, show how much is left, and announce the sell-out.
- **Why:** fixed supply creates fear of missing out [offers-p145]. The easiest version is honest capacity [offers-p148] [offers-p149], and showing how full you are also works as social proof [offers-p149]. Always sell out, and tell everyone you did [offers-p146]. In his fundraiser story, the charity cut the number of tickets and raised the price, and raised more than ever [offers-p134].
- **Example:** "50 Stay & Play packages at launch price. 31 left." (only if the count is real)

### 11. Urgency

- **Do:** give a dated deadline that is real: a price step, the end of a first-access window, a room-block release.
- **Why:** urgency is about time, scarcity about quantity [offers-p152]. A large share of sales arrive in the final hours [offers-p154]. Dates must be real or credibility suffers [offers-p154]. Seasonal wrappers and price-change notices create honest urgency [offers-p155] [offers-p156].
- **Example:** "Launch price ends Sunday, December 6, at midnight. Then $349."

### 12. The call to action

- **Do:** one clear next step, said and shown, with a reason to act now and what happens after the click.
- **Why:** a good CTA has what to do and reasons to do it now; clear language, such as "call now" rather than "don't delay" [leads-p66] [leads-p67]. Any reason beats no reason [leads-p69]. Show what happens next so the click meets expectations [goated-p23] [goated-p24].
- **Example:** "Book Stay & Play, $299. Next screen: pick 'Stay & Play for Two' in our Givebutter checkout."

### 13. Price presentation

- **Do:** present the best package first, anchor it with something bigger, and name tiers so each buyer knows which one is theirs.
- **Why:** present the premium offer first [mm-p49]; an anchor several times the price makes the main offer look better [mm-p86] [pricing-p54]. Make the contrast between options large [mm-p49]. Name tiers so customers see which is for them [pricing-p23]. There are four ways to express the same discount, worth testing [mm-p48] [mm-p49]. If customers buy once, make the purchase big [mm-p56].
- **Example:** "High Roller Table for eight. Stay & Play for two. VIP. General admission." in that order.

### 14. Guarantees and risk reversal

- **Do:** state the policy loudly, with its reason; reverse whatever risk you can afford to.
- **Why:** risk is the biggest objection, and the strength of a guarantee can change conversion a lot [offers-p169]. Always state your guarantee, even if you do not have one [offers-p170]. Anti-guarantees ("all sales final") suit consumable things and need a convincing reason [offers-p172] [offers-p181]. Name the guarantee something memorable [offers-p174].
- **Example:** "Every ticket funds the shelter, so sales are final. Can't come? Give your tickets to a friend at no cost."

### 15. Naming

- **Do:** name the offer with three to five of: a reason, the avatar, the goal, a time interval, a container word.
- **Why:** the MAGIC formula [offers-p188] [offers-p189] [offers-p190]. "Getaway" and "Experience" are on his container list [offers-p190]. Rhyme and alliteration help but are optional [offers-p190] [offers-p191]. Name bonuses the same way [offers-p193].
- **Example:** "Huntsville New Year's Getaway for Two."

### 16. Warm and cold audiences

- **Do:** treat people who gave permission (past attendees, golf supporters, donors) differently from strangers on Meta.
- **Why:** warm audiences gave you permission to contact them; cold ones did not, and that changes how you advertise [leads-p74]. Lookalike audiences built from your customer list, then filters, are how he targets cold traffic [leads-p194] [leads-p195]. Warm outreach should start with a personal note and an offer [leads-p85] [leads-p88]. Cold traffic needs more people to see the offer because fewer respond [leads-p191].
- **Example:** golf supporters get a personal text from the host; Meta gets a lookalike of the golf list.

### 17. Follow-up and nurture

- **Do:** follow up fast, often and personally, and remind people to show up.
- **Why:** show rates rise with availability, speed, personalisation and volume of follow-up [nurture-p11]. Automated reminders at fixed points before the appointment, labelled as automated [nurture-p35]. For warm lists, a limited-time push with countdowns, sales updates and a sold-out notice [fastcash-p13] [fastcash-p14]. People need reminding more than teaching [leads-p143].
- **Example:** "12 Stay & Play packages left. The room block releases Friday."

### 18. Upsells, downsells and referrals in the ticket funnel

- **Do:** offer the next thing the buyer will want, give a way to say yes for less, and ask who else is coming.
- **Why:** money models are sequences of offers [mm-p20]; classic upsells solve the problem the first purchase creates [mm-p71], and buyers spend most during a short excited window [mm-p72]. Downsells turn no into yes [mm-p23]. Ask for referrals at the moment of purchase [leads-p278]; always ask for the spouse [leads-p277].
- **Example:** after a GA purchase, "Add the room upstairs and skip the 1 AM rideshare."

## Where Hormozi doesn't fit

His books are written for businesses selling to buyers who want a result. A charity ticket is partly a purchase and partly a gift. What the books do and do not say:

1. **Donor motivation.** The books never analyse donors. The closest evidence is his $25,000-a-ticket fundraiser story, where he names status, goodwill, peer pressure, scarcity and bonuses as what drove people to knowingly overpay [offers-p132] to [offers-p135]. His "free, pick your price" lost chapter notes that people feel better paying when the money visibly helps the staff, and that paid "price ranges" led most people to pay above the midpoint so as not to seem cheap [lost-p108] [lost-p109]. Both suggest the mission works as a status and goodwill lever. What the books do not say is whether, for cold traffic, the mission or the night should lead. My inference: for cold Meta traffic lead with the night (the buyer's own outcome) and use the mission as the reason why and the status payoff; for the warm golf list, lead with the mission and first access.
2. **Tax-deductibility.** None of the books mentions it. Outside the books: for a ticket that buys goods or services, only the part above the fair market value of what the buyer receives is generally deductible, and a 501(c)(3) has written disclosure duties for such "quid pro quo" payments over $75 (IRS Publication 1771). With a hotel room in the package, the deductible part of Stay & Play may be small or nil. Do not claim "tax-deductible" on the page until the accountant gives the wording.
3. **Mission against self-interest.** His frameworks assume a self-interested buyer and price for profit; he even jokes that copying competitors' prices is running "a nonprofit with none of the benefits" [pricing-p18]. "Charge what it's worth" [offers-p65] and "raise your prices" [pricing-p22] still apply to a fundraiser, because profit is the money for the cause. But a below-cost launch package used to fill a hotel block is a business decision he would frame as acquisition, not charity (inference). The earlier offer review in the source folder records a 50-room-night threshold for unlocking event space; that is a project note to verify with the hotel, not evidence from the books.
4. **Guarantees on tickets.** His books push strong guarantees, but say anti-guarantees fit things that are consumed or lose value once used [offers-p172], and that high-cost fulfilment calls for conditional guarantees [offers-p171]. A New Year's Eve seat and a hotel room are both perishable and carry hotel cancellation terms. So a money-back guarantee is likely wrong here (inference); a bold "final sale, here's why" plus free transfer, plus a full refund if the Event is cancelled, follows his rule of stating the policy and the reason [offers-p170].
5. **Scarcity and trust.** He requires scarcity and deadlines to be real [offers-p146] [offers-p154]. For a nonprofit whose donors come back every year, a fake countdown costs more than it does for a store (inference). Only publish counts that come from the Givebutter inventory or the hotel.
6. **Giveaways and gaming law.** His giveaway chapter says to consult counsel [mm-p41]. The VSL script's notes say counsel must settle the prize and chip mechanics under Alabama law. The books add nothing beyond "ask a lawyer".
7. **Platform rules.** He warns that ad platforms reject outcome-plus-time claims that imply guarantees [offers-p190]. A social event rarely needs such claims; avoid "guaranteed" language in Meta ads.
